Who Was Fr. Seraphim Rose?

A Californian who lost the religion he was raised in, studied Chinese philosophy, walked into a Russian church out of curiosity, and died a hieromonk in the forest.

Updated 2026-09-23 · 9 min read

In short

Fr. Seraphim Rose (1934–1982) was born Eugene Dennis Rose in San Diego and raised Protestant. He abandoned religion as a student, studied Chinese philosophy at Pomona and Berkeley, and entered the Orthodox Church in San Francisco in 1962. He founded a brotherhood and a printing press, was tonsured a monk in 1970, ordained priest in 1977, and died at Platina, California, aged forty-eight.

A boy from San Diego

Eugene Dennis Rose was born in San Diego on 13 August 1934. His mother, Esther, came of a Norwegian family and was a Protestant; his father, Frank, of Dutch and French descent, had grown up Roman Catholic and became a Protestant for his wife's sake. Frank kept a sweet shop for a while, then worked for General Motors, and ended his working life as a janitor at a sports stadium. It was an ordinary American household, and the faith in it was ordinary Protestant faith.

The boy was not ordinary. He left San Diego High School in 1952 at the top of his class, and the people around him assumed he would go into science or mathematics. What he actually wanted, by the time he reached Pomona College in southern California, was something he would have found hard to explain to a careers master: not a profession, but an answer. He wanted to know what was really there.

The years he would not describe

At Pomona he put aside the religion of his childhood. It had struck him as thin, and he went looking for reality in Western philosophy instead. The search widened into the bohemian life of 1950s California. He read Nietzsche until the sentences had a kind of electricity in them, and he experimented deliberately with breaking down ordinary reasoning, on the theory that logic was the wall and unreason the door through it.

None of it worked. Hieromonk Damascene, who edited his early writings and later wrote his biography, records that when Eugene was asked years afterwards to describe that period, he would say only that he had been in hell. He drank. He shouted at the God he had publicly pronounced dead. This is worth stating plainly, because his later books are sometimes read as the work of a man who had never doubted anything. He had doubted everything, and it had very nearly finished him.

Chinese philosophy, and where it left him

He graduated from Pomona in 1956 with a degree in Oriental Languages, having learned Chinese, both ancient and modern, in order to read the sages without a translator between. In San Francisco he enrolled at the Academy of Asian Studies, whose dean was Alan Watts, then the most widely read interpreter of Zen in English. The teacher who mattered, though, was a quieter man: Gi-ming Shien, a Chinese philosopher whom Eugene regarded as a true representative of the tradition, and with whom he worked on a rendering of the Tao Te Ching from the ancient characters.

From Gi-ming Shien, and from the French writer René Guénon, he took a principle that would eventually decide his life: that a man ought to hold to the traditional, orthodox form of a religion rather than assemble a private one out of pieces he liked. He took his master's degree at Berkeley in 1961. And the wisdom of the pre-Christian philosophers, profound as he found it, left him unsatisfied — and unable to say why.

A Russian church in San Francisco

He went to an Orthodox service the way a scholar goes anywhere: to look at one more tradition. Describing it many years later, in a passage printed at the back of The Soul After Death, he said that something happened to him in that Russian church which had not happened in any Buddhist or other Eastern temple — something in his heart told him that this was home, and that the search was over. He did not understand the service. It was in a language he did not know. He kept going back.

What reached him slowly, over months of standing at the back and learning the customs, was an idea that his whole education had not prepared him for: that truth might not be an abstract thing sought by the mind at all, but something personal, even a Person, sought and loved by the heart. That, he wrote, was how he met Christ. He was received into the Orthodox Church on 25 February 1962, the Sunday of the Prodigal Son, in the Russian cathedral of the Mother of God “Joy of All Who Sorrow” in San Francisco. He was twenty-seven.

Archbishop John

The archbishop of San Francisco in those years was John Maximovitch, a small, stooped hierarch who had come by way of Shanghai and the Philippines, slept sitting up, went barefoot, and was regarded in his own lifetime as a wonder-worker. The Church canonised him in 1994 as St John of Shanghai and San Francisco. Eugene became his disciple. When Archbishop John began a course of theological classes in the city, Eugene attended every one and finished at the head of the class, although the lectures were delivered in Russian and he was the only American convert in the room.

Much of what is best in Fr. Seraphim's later writing came from this man rather than from any book. Lecturing to students at Santa Cruz twenty years afterwards, he was asked what it was about Archbishop John that drew people to him, and said the answer was always the same: he loved, and he gave himself away for others, and that is why things were shown to him.

God's revelation is given to something called a loving heart.

Fr. Seraphim Rose, God's Revelation to the Human Heart, §2 “Revelation” (St. Herman of Alaska Brotherhood, Second Edition, 2014)

A bookshop and a letterpress

In 1963, with a young Russian named Gleb Podmoshensky and with Archbishop John's blessing, Eugene founded a brotherhood named after St Herman of Alaska, the monk who had come from Valaam to Kodiak Island in 1794 and is counted the first saint canonised in America. The following year the two of them opened an Orthodox bookshop in San Francisco. In January 1965 they brought out the first number of a small bimonthly magazine, The Orthodox Word, setting and printing it themselves on a simple letterpress.

That magazine is the key to the man. He was never a professional theologian and held no academic post after Berkeley. He was, in the most literal sense, a publisher: a man who translated lives of saints out of Russian, set the type, read the proofs, and posted the result to a few hundred subscribers. Almost everything he wrote appeared there first, in instalments, written for readers many of whom he knew by name.

The forest at Platina

Archbishop John died in 1966. The two brothers, who had his blessing for the plan, began looking for land in the wilderness of northern California, and in 1969 they moved to a secluded mountainside near the small town of Platina, carrying the printing equipment up with them. On 27 October 1970 both were tonsured as monks. Eugene was given the name Seraphim, after the Russian ascetic St Seraphim of Sarov; Gleb was named for St Herman.

Ordination followed in 1977 — deacon in January, priest in April, at the hands of Bishop Nektary of Seattle, a disciple of the Optina elders. From then on he was a hieromonk, a monk who is also a priest. His last years were not the solitude he had gone to the mountain to find: letters arrived in sacks, and people came, and he answered them. A short and sudden illness took him on 2 September 1982. He was forty-eight years old.

Why a Protestant reader might find him worth reading

There are Orthodox writers who address Protestant readers from a considerable height, as if calling down from a wall. Fr. Seraphim is not one of them, and the reason is biographical. He had stood where such a reader stands. He was raised in a Protestant home and left it, not out of indifference, but out of a demand for something more real than he believed he had found there. He knew from the inside what it costs to want the truth badly enough to walk away from the faith you were handed.

His family never followed him. His mother remained a Protestant to the end, and his counsel to converts on this subject ran in the opposite direction from what people expect of him. Writing on 27 November 1980 to a catechumen distressed by the name-calling she had met in Orthodox books, he told her there was no reason she could not call her Protestant and Roman Catholic relatives Christians and be on good terms with them, recognising that they had at least faith in Christ in common, and that she should “live in peace especially with our own families”.

To another correspondent, a catechumen preparing for baptism in May 1979, he wrote a single sentence that sits oddly with his reputation for severity. It is the plainest thing he ever said about why a person should become Orthodox at all, and it is probably the fairest test to apply to any convert — or, for that matter, to him.

You are coming to Orthodoxy to receive Christ, and this you should never forget.

Fr. Seraphim Rose, letter of 3 May 1979, quoted in Hieromonk Damascene, Father Seraphim Rose: His Life and Works, ch. 84 (St. Herman Press, 2003)

A note on what to call him

One practical matter, because a reader meets it within about ten seconds of searching his name. Fr. Seraphim is widely venerated, and it is common to see him written of as “St Seraphim of Platina”. That title runs ahead of the Church. He was locally canonised in the Georgian diocese of Akhalkalaki in February 2023, but a local glorification does not bind the wider Church, and in the Russian Orthodox Church Outside Russia — the jurisdiction in which he lived and served — the question remains open.

Matters have been moving. In December 2025 the ROCOR Synod of Bishops established a commission, headed by Bishop James of Sonora, to study his life, legacy and veneration. In May 2026 the Synod was reported to have approved the opening of canonisation proceedings, although that report should be treated as provisional. As this page is written, in September 2026, the matter has not concluded.

For that reason this parish writes “Fr. Seraphim Rose” or “Hieromonk Seraphim (Rose)”, which is what he was called in his lifetime and what the Church's own documents still call him. The process has also drawn public criticism from some Orthodox writers, who fear his name is being attached to causes he did not take up. Readers who want the current position should check it with the Synod rather than rely on any web page, this one included, which may have been written before the next decision was taken.

Whatever is decided, the books will be what they have always been: the work of a man who looked very hard for the truth, in places most people never look, and who believed at the end that the truth had found him.

Common questions

What was Fr. Seraphim Rose's real name?

He was born Eugene Dennis Rose in San Diego, California, on 13 August 1934, and used that name until his monastic tonsure on 27 October 1970, when he received the name Seraphim after St Seraphim of Sarov. Books written before 1970, such as Nihilism, are published under “Eugene (Fr. Seraphim) Rose” for that reason. After his ordination in 1977 he was correctly styled Hieromonk Seraphim.

Was Fr. Seraphim Rose raised Orthodox?

No. He was raised Protestant in San Diego; his mother was a Protestant and his father, brought up Roman Catholic, became a Protestant when he married. Eugene rejected that faith as a student and spent several years in philosophical despair before entering an Orthodox church in San Francisco out of curiosity. He was received into the Orthodox Church in February 1962, at twenty-seven.

Is Fr. Seraphim Rose a saint?

That is not settled. He was locally canonised in a Georgian diocese in February 2023, and he is widely venerated by individual Orthodox Christians. Within the Russian Orthodox Church Outside Russia, a Synodal commission was established in December 2025 to study his life and veneration, and proceedings were reported to have been opened in May 2026. Until the Church concludes, “St Seraphim of Platina” is premature; write Fr. Seraphim Rose, and check the current status.

What did Fr. Seraphim Rose write?

His best-known books are The Soul After Death (1980) and Orthodoxy and the Religion of the Future (1975). Published after his death were God's Revelation to the Human Heart, The Place of Blessed Augustine in the Orthodox Church, Genesis, Creation and Early Man, and Nihilism, written before his tonsure. He also edited The Orthodox Word from 1965 and translated a great deal of Russian hagiography into English.

Who was Archbishop John Maximovitch to him?

Archbishop John Maximovitch was the Russian hierarch of San Francisco who received the young convert as a disciple, blessed the founding of the St Herman of Alaska Brotherhood in 1963 and the publishing work that followed, and blessed the eventual move to the wilderness. He died in 1966 and was canonised in 1994 as St John of Shanghai and San Francisco. Fr. Seraphim regarded him as the living argument for everything he later wrote.
