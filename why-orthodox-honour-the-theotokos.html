The Mother of God and the saints

Why Orthodox Christians Honour the Mother of God

The title Theotokos was defined to protect the truth about Christ; the honour Orthodox Christians give his mother follows from that, and stops short of worship.

Updated 2026-09-23 · 9 min read

In short

Orthodox Christians honour Mary as Theotokos, the one who gave birth to God in the flesh. The Council of Ephesus defined that title in 431 to protect the truth that Jesus Christ is one person, God and man. The honour given her is veneration, not worship; worship belongs to God alone. Orthodoxy asks her prayers and does not receive the Roman Catholic dogma of the Immaculate Conception defined in 1854.

The question a visitor asks first

A visitor arrives at the cathedral in Kovan on a Saturday evening, part-way through the vigil. The choir is singing something that repeats, and the repeated line is addressed to a woman. People are queueing to kiss her icon. If that visitor grew up in an evangelical church, a question forms before the service ends, and it is not a frivolous one: has something been placed beside Christ?

The question deserves an answer rather than a reassurance. Most people who raise it are guarding something they are right to guard, namely that Jesus Christ alone saves and that no creature may stand where he stands. Orthodox Christians say the same thing, in those words, every week. So the reply is not that the visitor has misheard, but that the words mean something particular, and the Church chose them for a reason.

Singapore makes the question ordinary rather than academic. Families here are often mixed: a Methodist mother, a Catholic uncle, a daughter who has started attending an Orthodox parish, all at the same table on Christmas Day. What is said about the mother of Christ in that room matters more than what is said in a seminar, and it deserves the same care.

The title was defined to protect Christ

Theotokos is Greek, usually rendered God-bearer, or the one who gave birth to God. It came before the Church as a live dispute in the fifth century. Nestorius, Archbishop of Constantinople, preferred Christotokos, Christ-bearer, because he thought the older title suggested that the divinity of the Son had a beginning in Mary's womb. He was trying to protect something true.

Cyril of Alexandria answered that a mother does not give birth to a nature; she gives birth to a person. The person born in Bethlehem is the eternal Son of God, who at that moment had taken flesh from her. To withhold the title is therefore to imply two sons, one eternal and one born, which is precisely what the Church could not accept. The Council of Ephesus in 431 settled the matter in Cyril's favour.

So the word is a statement about Christ before it is a statement about Mary. It says that the child in the manger is God. Much of the Reformation kept the title for that reason; the Lutheran Formula of Concord of 1577 confesses her as the mother of God in exactly this christological sense. When an Orthodox choir sings Theotokos, the subject of the sentence is her Son.

What Luke 1:48 asks of its readers

Scripture puts a prediction in Mary's own mouth. In the Magnificat she says, “for behold, henceforth all generations will call me blessed” (Luke 1:48, RSV). A few verses earlier Elizabeth, filled with the Holy Spirit, greets her: “Blessed are you among women” (Luke 1:42, RSV). The greeting and the prophecy sit inside the same chapter, spoken before the child is born.

Orthodox hymnography reads that verse as an instruction rather than a curiosity. Generations were to call her blessed, and the Church's liturgical books are, among other things, the record of generations doing so. Whether that is the right way to read the line is a fair question. It is a question about how to obey a verse, though, not about whether the verse is there.

There is also the scene at the Cross, where Christ gives his mother to the disciple and the disciple to his mother (John 19:26-27). Christians have long read that as more than a domestic arrangement made by a dying son. The woman given to the beloved disciple is given, in him, to the Church.

Veneration and worship are two different acts

This distinction is not a modern apology invented under pressure; it was made in council. At the Second Council of Nicaea in 787 the Church defined that holy images are to be given “due salutation and honourable reverence, not indeed that true worship of faith which pertains alone to the divine nature”. Two Greek words carry the weight: proskynesis, the honour paid to a person, and latreia, worship, which belongs to God and to nobody else.

A practical test is easier to check than a definition. In an Orthodox church the Eucharist is never offered to a saint, and there is no liturgy addressed to the Mother of God. The Divine Liturgy is offered to the Father, through the Son, in the Holy Spirit; she is commemorated within it as the first and best member of the Church. Nothing whatever is sacrificed to her.

Kissing an icon belongs to the same order of act as kissing a photograph of a mother who has died, which nobody mistakes for worship. Orthodox Christians bow before icons, kiss them, and light candles in front of them. What they do not do is offer them what is due to God.

English makes the confusion worse than Greek does. Until quite recently the word worship carried a broader sense in English: the Book of Common Prayer of 1662 has the bridegroom say to his bride, with my body I thee worship, and English magistrates are still addressed as Your Worship. Greek keeps two separate words for two separate things, and the councils used them precisely.

What the Orthodox Church holds about her

Set out plainly, Orthodoxy holds that Mary is Theotokos; that she is Ever-Virgin, a title used of her in Christian antiquity and taken up by the Fifth Ecumenical Council in 553; that she is all-holy, Panagia, and committed no personal sin; that at the end of her life she fell asleep and was taken to her Son, which the Church keeps on 15 August as the Dormition; and that she prays now for the Church on earth.

Equally worth stating is what the list does not contain. She is a creature. She was saved by her Son and said so: “my spirit hath rejoiced in God my Saviour” (Luke 1:47, KJV). Orthodoxy has never taught that she is a source of grace independent of Christ, or an addition to the Trinity, or a rival court of appeal. Her whole significance lies in whose flesh she gave.

Her role in the Gospel of John shows the pattern Orthodoxy claims she still follows. At the wedding in Cana she notices that the wine has run out, brings the need to her Son, and then turns to the servants with her last recorded words in Scripture: “Whatsoever he saith unto you, do it” (John 2:5, KJV). She points away from herself. That is what Orthodox Christians believe she is doing now.

What Orthodoxy does not hold: the Immaculate Conception

Many Protestant readers assume that Orthodox and Roman Catholic teaching about Mary are the same thing under two labels. They are not, and this is the clearest place where they part. On 8 December 1854, in the bull Ineffabilis Deus, Pope Pius IX defined that Mary was preserved free from every stain of original sin from the first instant of her conception. The Orthodox Church does not receive that dogma.

One reason is that the question it answers is framed by a Western account of the Fall. Where the Latin tradition came to speak of inherited guilt, Orthodoxy speaks of inherited mortality and corruption: a wounded nature rather than a charge on a record. If what descends from Adam is death rather than guilt, the difficulty the 1854 definition was built to remove does not arise in the same shape, and no exemption is required to keep her pure.

Archbishop John Maximovitch, the hierarch who blessed the founding of Fr. Seraphim Rose's brotherhood, put a second objection. He argued that the dogma belittles the Mother of God rather than exalting her, because it removes her struggle; a holiness that could never have been otherwise is not a victory won. Orthodox tradition holds that she was sanctified and kept from personal sin, and that this was a life lived in freedom, not a condition installed in advance.

A third reason concerns authority. Orthodoxy settles doctrine in council, received by the whole Church over time, and a definition issued by a single see in 1854 is not such a settlement. Orthodox hymns do call her most pure and spotless, and the Church keeps the feast of the Conception of St Anne on 9 December; neither carries the meaning defined in 1854. The difference is real and worth knowing before anyone argues about it.

The line that troubles visitors most

One refrain unsettles newcomers more than any other, and it is better faced than smoothed over. Through the canon at matins the choir sings, “Most Holy Theotokos, save us.” Heard cold, in English, that sounds like the very thing the objection fears.

What the phrase means, and what Orthodox writers consistently say it means, is: by your prayers, obtain salvation for us from your Son. The verb carries that range in Scripture. Paul writes that he becomes all things to all men “that I might by all means save some” (1 Corinthians 9:22, KJV), and tells Timothy that by watching his doctrine he shall “save thyself, and them that hear thee” (1 Timothy 4:16, KJV). Nobody reads those verses as a rival to Christ.

Honesty requires one more sentence. Compressed liturgical shorthand is hard on a stranger, and a stranger cannot be blamed for hearing it wrongly the first time. A parish that sings that line owes its visitors the explanation without waiting to be asked twice.

Fr. Seraphim Rose made a related point about arguments in general, and he made it against his own side rather than against anyone else's. A parish can be word-perfect about every hymn in its books and still fail the person standing at the back of the church, wondering whether it is safe to ask a question.

Being correct is the external side of Christianity. It's important, but not of first importance.

Fr. Seraphim Rose, talk on simplicity (1979), in Hieromonk Damascene, Father Seraphim Rose: His Life and Works, ch. 87 (St. Herman of Alaska Brotherhood)

An invitation rather than an argument

Fr. Seraphim Rose, the American hieromonk whom many enquirers read on their way towards Orthodoxy, was raised Protestant; his mother was a Protestant and his father a former Roman Catholic who had become one. When converts wrote to him armed with sharp arguments for their new faith, he told them to live at peace with their families, and he put part of the blame for Protestant misunderstanding where Orthodox Christians least like to find it.

He also thought that doctrinal accuracy, real as its value is, was not the first thing to be said about a Christian life. A parish that can defend every hymn and cannot love a visitor has answered the smaller question.

None of this asks anyone to agree by the end of the page. It asks only that the words be heard as the Church means them: a title that guards the Incarnation, an honour that Scripture itself predicted, and worship kept for God alone. The vigil is on Saturday evening, and questions afterwards are welcome.

perhaps it is our fault for not living and teaching the Orthodox Faith in a way they can understand

Fr. Seraphim Rose, letter of 27 November 1980, in Hieromonk Damascene, Father Seraphim Rose: His Life and Works, ch. 84 (St. Herman of Alaska Brotherhood)

Common questions

Do Orthodox Christians worship the Virgin Mary?

No. Orthodoxy distinguishes veneration, the honour given to a person, from worship, which belongs to God alone. The Second Council of Nicaea in 787 defined that difference in so many words. The practical test is what happens in church: the Eucharist is offered to the Holy Trinity and never to a saint, and there is no liturgy addressed to the Mother of God. She is honoured and asked to pray.

What does the word Theotokos actually mean?

It is Greek for God-bearer, or the one who gave birth to God. The Council of Ephesus affirmed it in 431 against Nestorius, who preferred Christ-bearer. The argument was about Christ, not about Mary: a mother gives birth to a person rather than to a nature, and the person born of her is the eternal Son of God who had taken flesh. The title guards the unity of Christ.

Does the Orthodox Church accept the Immaculate Conception?

No. The dogma defined by Pope Pius IX in 1854 holds that Mary was preserved from every stain of original sin from the first instant of her conception. Orthodoxy does not receive it, partly because it does not share the Western account of inherited guilt that the definition presupposes, partly because it was defined by one see rather than received in council. Orthodoxy still confesses her as all-holy and without personal sin.

Why do Orthodox hymns say “Most Holy Theotokos, save us”?

The line asks her to obtain salvation for us by her prayers to her Son. Scripture uses the verb in a similar stretched sense: Paul hopes to save some, and tells Timothy that he will save himself and his hearers. The phrase is liturgical shorthand, and it is fair to say that a visitor hearing it for the first time cannot be expected to catch that on their own.

Is honouring the Mother of God found in the Bible?

Orthodox Christians point to Luke 1, where Elizabeth calls her blessed among women and Mary herself says that all generations will call her blessed. They also point to the Cross, where Christ entrusts her to the beloved disciple. Whether those passages require the honour the Orthodox Church gives her is a genuine question, but the practice grows out of the text rather than around it.
