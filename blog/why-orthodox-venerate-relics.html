The Mother of God and the saints

Why Orthodox Christians Venerate Relics

Every Orthodox altar has a martyr's bone in it. Here is the reasoning behind a practice most Protestant visitors find genuinely strange.

Updated 2026-09-23 · 9 min read

In short

Orthodox Christians venerate relics, the bodily remains of saints, because they hold that the Incarnation ended any division between matter and holiness. Scripture records a corpse revived by Elisha's bones and cloths from Paul's body healing the sick. Relics are placed in every altar, and the Liturgy is served on a cloth containing one. Veneration is honour, not worship, which belongs to God alone.

There are bones in the altar

Start with the fact rather than the argument. In an Orthodox church, somewhere in or beneath the altar table, there are fragments of the bones of a martyr. On the table itself lies a cloth called the antimension, signed by the bishop, with a relic sewn into it; without that cloth a priest may not serve the Liturgy at all. Every Sunday, the bread and wine are set down on a piece of a dead Christian.

Stated that baldly, it sounds macabre, and there is no point pretending otherwise to a visitor who grew up in a church with clear windows and a wooden communion table. It is one of the practices that divides Orthodox instinct from Protestant instinct most sharply, and it will not be made palatable by softening the description.

What follows is the reasoning, laid out so that a reader can weigh it. It has three parts: what Scripture reports, what the Incarnation is taken to imply about matter, and what the Church has actually done since the second century. The abuses come in too, because leaving them out would be dishonest.

The objection is old, and it was raised at the time

The worry is not a Reformation invention. In the account of the death of Polycarp of Smyrna, written by his own congregation in the middle of the second century, the authorities are urged not to hand over his body, in case the Christians abandon the crucified one and begin to worship this one instead. The suspicion arrived with the practice, on day one.

In its modern form the objection is stronger and better aimed. It runs roughly so: Scripture nowhere commands the veneration of remains; the Law of Moses treated contact with a corpse as defiling; God deliberately hid the grave of Moses so that Israel would have no shrine; and the history of relics in Christendom is a history of fraud, money and credulity. Behind all of it stands the simplest point of all, which is that a bone is a bone.

None of that is unreasonable, and an Orthodox reply that treats the objection as mere squeamishness has replied to nothing. The response offered here accepts the historical charges, which are true and in places worse than commonly supposed, and disputes only the conclusion usually drawn from them. Fraud in the handling of a thing is an argument about the handlers.

Two passages Christians have always noticed

The first is in 2 Kings. A burial is interrupted by raiders, and the mourners throw the body into the nearest grave, which happens to be the prophet Elisha's. “And when the man was let down, and touched the bones of Elisha, he revived, and stood up on his feet” (2 Kings 13:21, KJV). The narrator reports it and moves on, with no commentary and no warning attached.

Acts supplies the second. “And God wrought special miracles by the hands of Paul: so that from his body were brought unto the sick handkerchiefs or aprons, and the diseases departed from them” (Acts 19:11-12, KJV). Earlier in the same book the sick are carried into the street so that Peter's shadow might fall on them (Acts 5:15), and in the Gospels a woman is healed by touching the hem of Christ's garment.

These passages do not command anything, and Orthodox Christians should not claim that they do. What they do is describe a world in which holiness is not sealed inside a person's soul but reaches what they touch and what remains of them. Orthodoxy takes that description seriously instead of treating it as a set of oddities.

Why Orthodoxy will not treat matter as inert

The deeper reason is the Incarnation. Christianity does not teach that God rescued souls out of a material world; it teaches that God took flesh, was carried in a womb, ate fish, was nailed to wood and rose bodily. After that, the idea that matter is spiritually neutral becomes very hard to hold, and Orthodoxy has never tried to hold it.

That same conviction runs through everything else Orthodox Christians do. Water is blessed and people are immersed in it. Oil is blessed and the sick are anointed with it. Bread and wine are offered and received as the Body and Blood of Christ. Paul tells the Corinthians that their bodies are “the temple of the Holy Ghost” (1 Corinthians 6:19, KJV), which is said of the body, not of the mind.

Relics follow from that, rather than standing on their own. If a body was a temple of the Holy Spirit during a saint's life, and if that body will be raised, then it is not simply rubbish when the soul departs. It is a Christian awaiting the resurrection. Honouring it is of a piece with everything else in the room.

What the Christians of Smyrna did

The earliest clear account is the one already mentioned. After Polycarp was killed, around the middle of the second century, his congregation gathered his bones, describing them as more precious than exquisite jewels and more purified than gold, and laid them in a fitting place. They then planned to meet there each year, on the anniversary of his death, with joy.

In the same document the congregation draws the distinction on which the whole practice depends, and draws it without being prompted. Christ, as the Son of God, they adore; the martyrs they love as disciples and followers of the Lord. Two different words for two different acts, written down by people who had watched their bishop die a few weeks earlier.

That text matters for the present argument because it is early, it is not defensive theology, and it shows the practice and its safeguard arriving together. Whatever grew up later, this is what the thing looked like at the start.

The altar, and why the relic is under it

The placing of relics in the altar is not decoration but an argument in furniture. John writes: “I saw under the altar the souls of them that were slain for the word of God” (Revelation 6:9, KJV). Orthodox and other ancient churches built their altars over the graves of martyrs, and where there was no grave they brought a relic to the table.

Eventually the practice was made a rule. The Second Council of Nicaea in 787 ordered that relics be placed, with the accustomed prayers, in churches that had been consecrated without them, and directed that a bishop who consecrated a church without relics be deposed. Behind the sharpness of the canon is a simple conviction: the Church is built on people who died for Christ, and the building should say so.

An antimension carries the same logic down to the parish. It is a cloth bearing an icon of the burial of Christ, containing a relic, signed by the diocesan bishop and given to a particular community. The priest opens it before the Liturgy and folds it away afterwards. A parish serving on that cloth is saying, each week, that it is not a private religious society but part of one Church with a bishop and a memory.

The abuses were real, and admitting it costs nothing

By the late Middle Ages in the West, relics had become an industry. Pieces of the True Cross multiplied beyond any possibility, bodies were divided and sold, and pilgrimage money shaped what a church claimed to possess. In 1543 John Calvin published a treatise cataloguing the duplications with a cold accuracy that is still uncomfortable reading, and much of what he listed was indefensible.

The Christian East has no clean record here either. Fraud follows money wherever it goes, and an Orthodox writer who presents the abuses as a purely Western problem is not telling the truth. It is worth saying plainly that the Orthodox Church has no interest in defending a forgery and no doctrine that requires any particular relic to be genuine.

What the abuses establish is that the practice can be corrupted, which was never in doubt. They do not establish that the practice is corrupt in itself, any more than simony shows that ordination is wrong. Anything valuable can be sold by someone; the question of whether it was valuable in the first place has to be settled separately.

As for prejudices—these belong to people, not the Church.

Fr. Seraphim Rose, letter of 27 November 1980, in Hieromonk Damascene, Father Seraphim Rose: His Life and Works, ch. 84 (St. Herman of Alaska Brotherhood)

What veneration looks like on an ordinary day

The practice itself is undramatic. A reliquary is set out on a stand. People come forward in a queue, make the sign of the Cross, bow, kiss the case, and stand for a moment. Some ask the saint to pray for a sick relative. Then they go back to their places, and the service continues towards the Eucharist, which is offered to God alone.

No sacrifice is made to a relic and no prayer is addressed to the bone. The hymns of the day are addressed to Christ, with requests for the prayers of the saint whose memory is kept. If a visitor watched the whole service with a text in hand, the grammar would be consistent from beginning to end.

Something else is worth noticing in a city like Singapore, where many parishioners are far from the graves of their own families. Standing in front of the relics of a Christian who died in Asia Minor in the third century is a way of saying that the family is larger than the one that can be visited at Choa Chu Kang.

What is being claimed, and what is not

The claim is modest in shape, even if it is strange in practice. Orthodoxy holds that God works through created things because he has always worked through created things, that the bodies of the saints are not discarded matter, and that honouring them honours the Christ who lived in them. It does not hold that relics are magical, that they operate apart from faith, or that any individual relic must be believed genuine.

Fr. Seraphim Rose, an American convert who was raised Protestant and later lived as a hieromonk in the forests of northern California, thought that arguments were rarely what moved people towards the Church. What moved them, he said, was seeing something in Christians that they recognised as worth having, and he put the burden of that squarely on the Orthodox.

That seems the right note to end on. Nobody is asked to agree with this page. Anyone is welcome to come to the cathedral in Kovan on a feast day, watch what is done, and ask the priest afterwards why a church keeps bones on its altar. The answer given will be roughly the one above, at more length and with fewer footnotes.

If we have the true Christianity, there should be something in our midst that someone who sincerely loves the truth will see and want.

Fr. Seraphim Rose, lecture “Orthodoxy in the USA”, The Orthodox Word no. 94 (1980), quoted in Hieromonk Damascene, Father Seraphim Rose: His Life and Works, ch. 86

Common questions

Where does the Bible support the veneration of relics?

Scripture does not command it, and Orthodox Christians should not claim that it does. What Scripture reports is a man revived by contact with Elisha's bones, cloths carried from Paul's body healing the sick, Peter's shadow falling on the ill, and a woman healed by touching the hem of Christ's garment. Orthodoxy takes those reports as describing how holiness actually behaves rather than as curiosities.

What is the antimension?

It is a cloth laid on the altar table, bearing an icon of the burial of Christ and containing a relic, signed by the diocesan bishop and issued to a particular parish. The Liturgy is celebrated on it, and a priest may not serve without one. It ties every parish altar to a martyr and to a bishop, which is precisely the point of it.

Isn't venerating bones just superstition?

It can become superstition, and Orthodox priests correct it when it does. The practice as the Church teaches it is honour paid to the body of a Christian in whom the Holy Spirit dwelt and which will be raised. Nothing is sacrificed to a relic and no prayer is addressed to it. The distinction between veneration and worship was drawn in the second century and has been maintained since.

What about fake relics and the medieval relic trade?

Those abuses were real. Calvin's 1543 treatise catalogued duplicated relics with accuracy, and the Christian East has had frauds of its own. No Orthodox teaching requires any particular relic to be authentic, and the Church has nothing to gain from defending a forgery. The abuse of a practice shows that it can be corrupted, not that it was wrong to begin with.

Why are relics placed under the altar?

Early Christians celebrated the Eucharist over the graves of martyrs, and Revelation 6:9 describes the souls of the slain as being under the altar. The Second Council of Nicaea in 787 required relics to be placed in churches consecrated without them. The arrangement says that the Church rests on those who died for Christ rather than on the people currently occupying the building.
