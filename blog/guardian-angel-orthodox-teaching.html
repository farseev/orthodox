Guardian Angels in Orthodox Teaching: What the Church Really Says

The Church believes that God gives each Christian an angel, and it prays to that angel daily. It also refuses to turn the angel into a lucky charm or a private fortune-teller.

Updated 2026-10-26 · 7 min read

In short

The Orthodox Church teaches that God appoints a guardian angel to each Christian, usually understood as given at baptism, to guide, protect and pray for the person. The belief rests on Scripture (Matthew 18:10, Psalm 90, Acts 12, Hebrews 1:14), on the Fathers and on the Church's prayers. Angels are servants of God, not independent powers, and they can be driven away by persistent sin.

Does every Christian have a guardian angel?

In Orthodox teaching, yes: the Church holds that God appoints an angel to accompany each believer, and in the usual understanding the angel is given at baptism. This is a firm and ancient belief, though the Church has not defined it as a formal dogma with a precise explanation of how it works. We know the outline, not the mechanics. Angels are created spiritual beings, intelligent and free, who serve God and help people. The Greek word angelos, like the Hebrew mal'ak, means messenger, and the name describes a task, not a nature.

Orthodox services speak of the angel in the most ordinary terms. At every Great Litany the deacon asks for an angel of peace, a faithful guide, a guardian of our souls and bodies. The rite of baptism includes a prayer that God would grant the newly baptised a bright angel as a faithful guardian. The belief is therefore not an optional piece of piety but part of the way the Church prays. For the wider picture of the spiritual world, see Angels, demons and the unseen world.

What does Scripture say about guardian angels?

The clearest text is the Lord's own warning about children: do not despise one of these little ones, because their angels in heaven always see the face of the Father (Matthew 18:10 listen in the Audio Bible). Many Fathers read this as direct evidence that individuals have angels. Psalm 90 in the Septuagint numbering, used often in Orthodox prayer, promises that God will give His angels charge over you to guard you in all your ways (Psalm 90:11 listen in the Audio Bible).

The Acts of the Apostles gives a vivid scene. When St Peter was freed from prison by an angel and knocked at the door of the house where Christians were praying, they at first refused to believe it was he and said it must be his angel (Acts 12:15 listen in the Audio Bible). The scene shows how natural the idea of a personal angel was among the first Christians. Finally, the Epistle to the Hebrews describes angels as ministering spirits sent to serve those who are to inherit salvation (Hebrews 1:14 listen in the Audio Bible). None of these passages explains everything, which is why the Church reads them alongside the Fathers, as in How the Church Fathers read Scripture.

What did the Church Fathers teach about guardian angels?

Origen, in the third century, wrote that every human soul is under the direction of an angel who is like a father, and St Basil the Great, in the fourth, taught that an angel is set over each believer so long as we do not drive him away by sin. Many later Fathers repeat the same idea, and it is also present in the liturgical hymns. The practical point is that the angel is not an automatic bodyguard. Sin, and a settled refusal to repent, grieves the angel and weakens our own attention to his help.

A further question has often been raised: does the guardian angel belong only to Christians? The Fathers were not unanimous in the details, and Orthodox writers often say that the Church speaks confidently about the angels of believers and prays that every person may be under God's care. Whatever the answer, nobody is outside God's providence, a theme taken up in Divine providence and coincidence. Orthodoxy also holds that angels have their own tasks over nations, cities and churches, which is why the Book of Revelation addresses an angel of each church.

How do Orthodox Christians pray to their guardian angel?

Most Orthodox prayer books include a short prayer to the guardian angel among the morning or evening prayers, asking the angel to protect, guide and present one's prayers to God. In Russian prayer books there is also a longer service, the Canon to the Guardian Angel, which many people read before Communion or on particular days. Mondays are dedicated to the angels in the weekly cycle of the Church, and the services of that day include hymns to them.

A simple practice is enough. At the start or end of the day say a short prayer to your angel, and add your own words: ask him to help you resist a temptation, or to guard a particular person you worry about. The angel does not replace prayer to Christ, who is the centre, and it is Christ who gives the angel his work. For a beginner's outline of daily prayer, see How to pray in the Orthodox way. A person of a different Christian tradition may find that the practice recalls the same trust in the care of God.

When is the feast of the angels?

The principal feast is the Synaxis of the Archangel Michael and the other Bodiless Powers, on 8 November in the Church calendar. Churches that follow the Julian calendar, including the Russian Orthodox Church, keep it on what is 21 November on the civil calendar. A synaxis means a gathering, and the feast gathers the memory of all the angelic powers under their leader, St Michael. Other angelic feasts occur through the year.

You can find the exact dates for the current year on the church calendar, and the Sunday and festal services are listed on the Liturgy page. It is worth noting that Orthodox icons of angels do not show them as the winged infants of Western art. They are shown as dignified, young, bright figures, because the Church wants us to remember them as powerful servants of God and not as sentimental protectors. See Why Orthodox churches have icons.

What angels are not: avoiding superstition

The angel is not a lucky charm. A guardian angel does not guarantee comfort or success, and the saints who had the closest relationship with God suffered grievously. He is also not a source of private revelations on demand. The Church warns against trying to see, hear or summon angels, because a spiritual experience that is sought for its own sake is a classic entrance to what the tradition calls prelest, spiritual delusion. Not every voice or vision with an angelic look is from God, and St Paul himself warns that Satan disguises himself as an angel of light.

The healthy attitude is gratitude and ordinary prayer without curiosity. If someone claims angelic messages, tests them against the Gospel and the Church's teaching, and asks the confessor. See What is prelest? for how to recognise this, and Discernment of spirits. Angels also do not replace the saints. We ask both for their prayers, as explained in The communion of saints, and the departed are remembered in a different way, as in Why Orthodox Christians pray for the dead.

How can I live with the awareness of an angel?

A practical rule is to live as someone who is seen and accompanied. An angel does not look on with disapproval but with concern for your salvation. Some Orthodox writers suggest a short pause before an act you doubt: if my angel is here, what would he want me to do? This is not a method of divination but a way of keeping conscience awake. It fits the Gospel's own reminder that there is joy before the angels of God over one sinner who repents (Luke 15:10 listen in the Audio Bible).

It also helps to hear the Scriptures that speak of angels in context. The Audio Bible lets you listen to Matthew 18, Acts 12 and Hebrews 1 in a quiet moment: the Audio Bible. If you struggle with fear, with the sense of being watched by evil, or with a question about a particular experience, do not carry it alone. Speak to a priest, since personal pastoral questions are not for an article to settle.

Common questions

Does every person have a guardian angel in Orthodoxy?

Orthodox teaching holds that God gives each Christian a guardian angel, usually understood as given at baptism. The belief rests on Scripture, the Fathers and the Church's prayers, though the Church has not formulated a detailed dogma about it. Whether non-Christians also have one is not defined, and all people are under God's providence.

What does Matthew 18:10 say about angels?

In Matthew 18:10 Christ warns against despising the little ones, because their angels in heaven always see the face of His Father. Orthodox Fathers have read this as evidence that individuals, especially children, are watched over by angels. It does not explain how the angels work, but it affirms that they do.

How do I pray to my guardian angel?

Use the short prayer to the guardian angel in an Orthodox prayer book at morning or evening, and add your own words. Ask your angel to guard you from sin, guide your decisions and offer your prayers to God. Keep Christ at the centre, and treat the angel as a helper, not as a source of visions.

When is the feast of the angels in the Orthodox Church?

The main feast is the Synaxis of the Archangel Michael and the other Bodiless Powers on 8 November in the Church calendar, which falls on 21 November on the civil calendar for Churches using the Julian calendar. Mondays are also dedicated to the angels in the weekly cycle of services.

Can my guardian angel leave me?

The Fathers teach that sin does not make the angel hate us but can grieve him and dull our awareness of his help. St Basil the Great spoke of driving him away by sin. Repentance and confession restore the relationship. The Church holds that God does not abandon a person who turns back to Him.

Do Orthodox Christians believe in angels with wings and halos?

Orthodox icons usually show angels with wings and a halo as symbols of their speed in God's service and holiness, not as a literal anatomy. The Church teaches that angels are bodiless spiritual beings. The icons express their nature in a visual language and differ from the cherubs of Western art.
