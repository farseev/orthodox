The soul after death

Near-Death Experiences: An Orthodox Reading

Orthodoxy does not dismiss these experiences, and does not treat them as maps of the world to come; it asks a different question about them.

Updated 2026-09-23 · 9 min read

In short

Orthodox Christianity does not deny that near-death experiences happen or that they are sincerely reported. It declines to read them as descriptions of heaven. The reported being of light has no definite form and is identified according to each person's background, unlike the angels of Orthodox accounts; and the apostolic rule is to test the spirits rather than to trust an experience because it felt loving. The reserve is about interpretation, not about the person.

If this happened to you

A good many people reading this will have had something of their own to set against the discussion below. A cardiac arrest, a haemorrhage, a bad accident; and then a clarity that has never quite faded, a sense of being looked at with complete understanding, a reluctance to be sent back. Those memories are usually reported with unusual honesty, often by people who gain nothing by telling them and who have been careful not to overstate.

Nothing in Orthodox teaching requires anyone to say that such a person is lying, mad, or wicked. The caution that follows is about what an experience can be taken to establish, which is a different question from whether it occurred. It is worth reading it in that spirit, and worth saying at the outset that the right place to talk over your own experience is with a priest who knows you, not with an article.

What the reports describe

The modern discussion began in 1975 with Raymond Moody's Life After Life, followed by the work of Elisabeth Kubler-Ross and the studies of Karlis Osis and Erlendur Haraldsson. Moody assembled a composite model out of recurring elements: hearing oneself pronounced dead, a sensation of leaving the body and observing the room, a passage through a dark space often described as a tunnel, meetings with deceased relatives, a luminous presence, a rapid review of one's life, and a reluctant return.

Fr. Seraphim Rose took that literature seriously enough to write a book about it. The Soul After Death, published in 1980, was an attempt to say what the older Orthodox sources make of the phenomenon. He accepted that something real was being reported. His question was what region of reality it belonged to.

What Orthodoxy does not dispute

It is easy to miss how much common ground there is. The Church has always taught that the soul survives the death of the body and remains conscious, and the Fathers gathered in the book argue that its faculties become sharper once freed from the flesh rather than duller. The soul is met: our Lord says the beggar died and was carried by the angels to Abraham's bosom (Luke 16:22, RSV).

So an Orthodox reader has no reason to be disturbed by reports of awareness outside the body, and no stake in explaining them away as oxygen deprivation. Where the difficulty begins is with the further claim, made most confidently by the popular investigators rather than by the patients, that these accounts show us what awaits everyone and that death has nothing fearful in it.

The tunnel, and what it may not be

Fr. Seraphim's first objections were methodological rather than theological, and they hold up reasonably well. Moody's composite, he argued, was assembled from short excerpts rather than from whole accounts, and pressed rather different things into a single model: actual clinical death, and states that merely approach it.

The tunnel is his own example. Entering a tunnel is reported by people given ether and by people in no danger of dying, just as the rapid review of one's life is reported by people who nearly drown and recover without incident. Those elements may belong to some more general experience and be incidental to dying as such. That is a modest point, but it undercuts the habit of treating the whole sequence as a single map of the passage out of this life.

The being of light

The element Moody called the most profound is the appearance of a light that is recognised as a personal presence, full of warmth and understanding, which communicates without words and provokes the dying person to reflect without passing any judgment. Moody himself noted that the identification of this being depends on the person's religious background: some call it Christ, some an angel, some simply a presence, and in itself it has no recognisable form.

Fr. Seraphim answers by comparison rather than by assertion. Angels, in the Orthodox sources, are markedly more definite than this, both in appearance and in what they do. They come as young men in dazzling white, as they do in the Gospel accounts of the resurrection; they take the soul firmly by the arms and conduct it somewhere, and they do not stop to chat.

A formless presence that converses at length and screens a review of one's life does not match that description, and he declines to call it an angel on the strength of its warmth, recalling the apostle's warning that Satan himself is transformed into an angel of light (2 Corinthians 11:14, KJV). Rather than settle the matter by assertion, though, he first does what a writer in a hurry to win would not do, and leaves the question standing on the page.

Who — or what — are these 'beings of light'?

Testing the spirits

This is where the argument connects to something much older than the 1970s. The rule the Fathers apply to every remarkable religious experience is apostolic: beloved, do not believe every spirit, but test the spirits to see whether they are of God (1 John 4:1, RSV). The criterion is not the intensity of the experience or the goodness of the feelings it leaves behind. Ascetic writers call the failure to apply it prelest, spiritual delusion, and they are unanimous that a deception which announced itself as evil would deceive nobody.

That is precisely the weakness Fr. Seraphim identified in the investigators' own reasoning. Moody raised the possibility of deception only to dismiss it, on the grounds that a diabolical apparition would presumably counsel hatred and destruction. The patristic literature says the opposite: temptations of this kind arrive dressed as something good, which is why the Lives of Saints contain so many accounts of dying ascetics refusing to accept a consoling vision at face value. None of this amounts to a verdict on anybody's experience; it is a reason for not building a doctrine on one.

What such reports can establish

Fr. Seraphim's summary is more restrained than his reputation suggests. These accounts, he argued, can prove no more than the bare survival of the soul outside the body and the bare existence of a non-material reality, while telling us nothing reliable about the state of the soul beyond the first few minutes. Elisabeth Kubler-Ross's claim that this research would confirm what Christians had been taught for two thousand years he judged an exaggeration, noting that Moody himself said the accounts did not corroborate a conventional Christian picture of heaven.

He offered a second, diagnostic argument. What is missing from the modern accounts is as striking as what is present: the angels who come for the soul, any sense of judgment, and the sheer duration recorded in older sources, where such experiences last hours or days rather than minutes. From this he concluded that the modern reports touch only the anteroom of death, before anything is settled and while return by natural means is still possible.

All of that is argument, and he knew it was argument. In the preface to the book he separated the Church's teaching, which can be stated, from his own readings of particular contemporary reports, which he offered as his own and expected to be contested.

Concerning these interpretations, of course, there is room for a legitimate difference of opinion among Orthodox Christians.

Fr. Seraphim Rose, The Soul After Death, Preface (St Herman of Alaska Brotherhood, 2009 printing)

The comparison that gave most offence

The part of the book that causes most argument is Chapter Seven, where the modern accounts are set beside a very different literature: the Tibetan Book of the Dead, the writings of Emanuel Swedenborg, the astral plane of Theosophy, and the twentieth-century manuals of astral projection. Fr. Seraphim found the resemblance close, down to the luminous guides who meet the traveller and see him on his way.

His conclusion is a claim about a region and about a body of interpretation, not an accusation against patients in intensive care, and it is worth reading it that way. He is saying that the area just beyond the body has been described before, by people who went looking for it on purpose, and that the modern reports fit those older descriptions better than they fit the visions recorded in the Lives of Saints.

Orthodox writers were not alone in sounding an alarm about this literature, either. Fr. Seraphim quotes evangelical Protestant authors of the same period who warned that sincere Christians were identifying the being of light with Jesus Christ too readily and were poorly placed to notice if they were wrong. He thought their criticism under-equipped rather than wrong-headed. On the basic instinct that something here needed testing, they and he were on the same side. About the region itself, though, his own sentence is flat and unhedged.

These are, purely and simply, 'out-of-body' experiences, something well known especially in occult literature

Fr. Seraphim Rose, The Soul After Death, ch. 7, Conclusions (St Herman of Alaska Brotherhood, 2009 printing)

How to hold your own experience

For a person who has actually been through this, the pastoral advice in the Orthodox tradition is consistent and unspectacular. Do not treat what you saw as a revelation to be interpreted and taught. Do not seek to repeat it; the ascetic writers are severe about deliberately reaching for such states. Do not conclude from a warm reception that nothing is required of you now, and do not conclude from a frightening one that you are condemned. Tell it to your confessor, and then go on with the ordinary things: prayer, confession, communion, forgiving people, helping somebody.

Fr. Seraphim's own reserve is worth keeping in view here, because the book has often been quoted more confidently than its author wrote it. He offered his readings of particular modern cases as readings, and said plainly that Orthodox Christians might legitimately differ about them. Readers from other Christian traditions will differ further, and about more, and that is an honest place to leave the matter rather than a failure of nerve.

If you carry a memory of this kind, the Orthodox Church does not ask you to renounce it or to explain it. She asks of it what she asks of every striking experience, including the visions of her own saints: that it be tested, held humbly, and not allowed to stand in for the life of repentance it may have been given to interrupt. That is an invitation rather than a correction, and it is a conversation better had over tea than in a comment thread. If you are in Singapore, you are welcome to come and have it.

Common questions

Does the Orthodox Church say near-death experiences are demonic?

It does not issue a blanket verdict. Fr. Seraphim Rose argued that the modern reports describe the aerial region just beyond the body rather than heaven, and that the formless being of light cannot safely be identified as an angel. He treated his interpretations as open to legitimate disagreement among Orthodox Christians. The Church's settled rule is the apostolic one: test the spirits rather than accept or condemn an experience on the strength of how it felt.

Why is the being of light treated with suspicion?

Because of what it lacks rather than what it shows. Angels in Orthodox sources appear in a definite form, as young men in white, and they conduct the soul somewhere. The reported presence has no form, varies in identification with the person's religious background, and declines to judge. Fr. Seraphim set this beside the apostle's warning that Satan is transformed into an angel of light, and concluded that the identification cannot simply be assumed.

Is the tunnel evidence of the passage after death?

Fr. Seraphim thought it doubtful. He noted that entering a tunnel is commonly reported by people given anaesthetics who were never close to dying, as the rapid review of one's life is reported by people who nearly drown. Elements like these may belong to some broader experience and be incidental to death itself. That is a caution about building conclusions on a composite model, not a denial that people experience them.

What did Fr. Seraphim Rose think these experiences prove?

Very little on their own. He argued they establish no more than the bare survival of the soul outside the body and the existence of a non-material reality, and give no reliable information about the state of the soul beyond the first minutes. He called the claim that they confirm two thousand years of Christian belief an exaggeration, noting that the leading investigator himself said they did not corroborate a conventional Christian view of heaven.

I had an experience like this. What should I do?

Speak to a priest who knows you, rather than to the internet. The consistent counsel in the Orthodox tradition is not to seek such states, not to treat the memory as a teaching for others, and not to draw conclusions about your standing before God from it in either direction. What follows is ordinary Christian life: prayer, confession, communion, and attention to the people around you. Nobody will ask you to deny what you remember.

Do Protestant writers share any of these concerns?

Some do, and Fr. Seraphim said so. He quoted evangelical Protestant authors of the late 1970s who warned that sincere Christians were identifying the being of light with Christ too quickly and were in a poor position to notice a deception. He thought their criticisms lacked the older Christian material on the spirits to work with, but he treated their instinct as sound. The caution is not a point of division between Orthodox and Protestant Christians.
