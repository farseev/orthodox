The Nativity Fast: What It Is and How Orthodox Christians Keep It

Forty days before Christmas, the Church slows down and prepares. The Nativity Fast is shorter and gentler than Great Lent, and it is just as much about the heart.

Updated 2026-10-26 · 3 min read

In short

The Nativity Fast is the Orthodox preparation for Christmas. It lasts from 28 November to 6 January on the civil calendar (15 November to 24 December on the Church calendar) and ends with the Nativity of Christ on 7 January. The rule is lighter than Great Lent: meat and dairy are set aside throughout, fish is permitted on some days, and Wednesdays and Fridays are stricter. The fast is meant to prepare the heart for the feast, not to be a diet.

Why the Church fasts before Christmas

The Church does not keep a feast without preparing for it. Pascha has Great Lent, Christmas has the Nativity Fast, and the feasts of the Apostles and of the Dormition have theirs. Fasting before a feast is how the Church makes room for it: the body is quieter, the day has more silence in it, and attention goes to prayer and to people.

It is sometimes called Philip's Fast because it begins right after the feast of the holy Apostle Philip (27 November on the civil calendar). Its Western parallel, Advent, is an old relative, but it is not identical in shape or rule.

The dates

In 2026 the fast begins on 28 November and ends on the night of 6 January, with the Nativity Liturgy on 7 January. On the Church calendar the same days are 15 November to 24 December. It is 40 days long, in the biblical number of preparation.

The last days of the fast are marked by services that look ahead to the feast: the Sundays before Nativity commemorate the forefathers and the fathers of the Old Testament, and the Eve of the Nativity, 6 January, is a strict day.

What the rule asks

The usual rule is no meat, eggs or dairy through the whole fast. Fish is permitted on Saturdays and Sundays until 2 January, and on certain feasts such as St Nicholas. On weekdays the rule is stricter, and from 2 January to 5 January it is stricter still, with no fish. On the Eve, the Church traditionally asks for the strictest fast and, for those who are able, no food until the first star. Exactly what is allowed on a given weekday (oil, wine) is set out in the fasting page and varies a little between traditions.

This is the general rule. Practice differs between parishes and families, and the Church does not ask the sick, the elderly, children, pregnant and nursing mothers or those who travel to keep it as strictly. The most important step is to talk to your priest and ask him what your rule is, and then to keep that rule faithfully. See the fasting page of this site for the daily rule.

What changes in church

The services take on a different colour: the hymns and the readings look toward the feast, and from 2 January (20 December on the Church calendar) the Church sings the Forefeast hymns of the Nativity. The Sunday before Christmas is the Sunday of the Holy Fathers. The Sacraments of Confession and Communion are especially encouraged during the fast, so that one comes to Christmas reconciled and receives the Eucharist on the feast.

Keeping it without losing the point

The Fathers are firm that fasting is not only a matter of food. If you give up meat and gossip is unchanged, you have missed it. A simple way to keep the fast in proportion is to add something along with what is taken away: a daily prayer, a short reading from the Gospels, a visit to someone who is lonely, a gift to the parish or to someone in need.

If a rule is too heavy, a smaller one that is kept is better than a strong one that is dropped in the second week. Begin with what is possible, and ask the priest to bless it.

Common questions

When does the Nativity Fast start and end in 2026?

It starts on 28 November 2026 and ends on 6 January 2027 (15 November to 24 December on the Church calendar). The feast of the Nativity is on 7 January.

Can I eat fish during the Nativity Fast?

On the general rule, fish is permitted on Saturdays and Sundays and on some feast days before 2 January, but not on weekdays, and not in the last days before Christmas. Ask your priest for the rule that applies to you.

Is the Nativity Fast as strict as Great Lent?

No. It is shorter and lighter: the days on which fish, oil and wine are allowed are more numerous. It is still a fast, and it still asks for prayer, almsgiving and reconciliation.

Why do the Orthodox celebrate Christmas on 7 January?

Because the Russian Orthodox Church keeps the Church calendar (the Julian calendar), on which 25 December falls on 7 January on the civil calendar. The fixed feasts are the same days on the Church calendar; only the civil date differs.
