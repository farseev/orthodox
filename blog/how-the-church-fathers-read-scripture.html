Scripture, Tradition and authority

Reading Scripture with the Church Fathers

Why Orthodoxy reads the Bible inside a community stretched across twenty centuries, and what the Fathers were actually doing when they read.

Updated 2026-09-23 · 9 min read

In short

The Church Fathers read Scripture as one book whose subject is Christ, tested against the rule of faith handed down from the apostles. They read the Old Testament typologically, as the Lord himself did on the road to Emmaus. Orthodox Christians read with them, not because any Father was infallible, but because reading without a community stretched across time tends to lose its bearings without noticing.

Who counts as a Father

The phrase can sound like a club with a rope across the door. It is looser than that. The Fathers are the teachers whose writing the Church has found, over long use, to express her own mind: Irenaeus in Gaul, Athanasius and Cyril in Alexandria, the three Cappadocians, John Chrysostom in Antioch and Constantinople, Maximus the Confessor, John of Damascus, and others down to writers as recent as Ignatius Brianchaninov in the nineteenth century.

No list was ever promulgated and nobody sat an examination. What made a writer a Father was reception: his works kept being copied, read at the services, cited at councils and found useful by people trying to pray. That is a slow and rather unglamorous process. It is also, as it happens, much the same process that produced the canon of Scripture itself.

The rule of faith

The first thing the Fathers brought to Scripture was not a technique. It was a summary of the faith received at baptism, the regula fidei or rule of faith, which is the ancestor of the creeds. St Irenaeus sets it out around 180 in Against Heresies, book one, chapter ten: one God, one Christ incarnate, one Spirit, one coming judgement, confessed in the same terms by churches in Germany, Spain, Gaul, the East, Egypt and Libya.

In the preceding chapter he explains why this matters, with an image nobody forgets. A skilled artist makes a mosaic portrait of a king out of precious stones. Someone else takes the stones apart and refits them into the shape of a fox, then points to the jewels as proof that this is the king. Every piece is genuine. The arrangement is not.

That is his charge against the Gnostic readers of his day: each verse they cite is in the Bible, and the picture they assemble from the verses is not the one the apostles handed down. The rule of faith is what tells a reader whether he is looking at the king or at the fox. It is not an addition to Scripture. It is the shape Scripture takes when it is read whole.

Typology, or reading the Old Testament as Christ read it

On the road to Emmaus, “beginning with Moses and all the prophets, he interpreted to them in all the scriptures the things concerning himself” (Luke 24:27, RSV). The Fathers took that verse as both permission and instruction. The Old Testament is not a separate religion with a Christian appendix attached; it is one story, told in advance and in figures.

The apostles had already begun. St Paul says of Israel in the wilderness that these things happened to them as types, and identifies the rock that followed them with Christ (1 Corinthians 10:1-11). St Peter reads Noah's ark as a figure of baptism (1 Peter 3:20-21). The Lord reads the bronze serpent of Numbers as a figure of the Cross (John 3:14) and Jonah as a figure of the three days (Matthew 12:40).

Once you have noticed it, it is everywhere in the services. Paschal hymns sing the crossing of the Red Sea as baptism. The Great Canon of St Andrew of Crete, chanted in the first week of Great Lent, works through the whole Old Testament applying each figure to the soul of the person singing it. Orthodox typology is not principally an academic method. It is what the Church does aloud every year.

Alexandria: the sense beneath the letter

Two emphases developed and are usually labelled by city. Alexandria, a Greek intellectual capital with a long habit of reading Homer allegorically, produced readers who looked for the spiritual meaning underneath the surface of the text. Origen, in the third century, proposed three senses corresponding to body, soul and spirit, and read a great deal as pointing beyond itself.

That tradition continued through Didymus and, in a far more restrained form, through St Cyril of Alexandria, whose commentaries are among the treasures of the Church. At its best the Alexandrian instinct kept Christians from reading the Old Testament as the mere chronicle of a small nation. At its worst it could float away from the text altogether.

Reception here was uneven and it is worth saying so. Origen's exegetical influence was immense and much of it was absorbed gratefully, while a body of doctrine associated with his later followers was condemned in the sixth century. St Vincent of Lérins, writing in 434, chose him as the chief example of how a gifted and holy teacher can be admired and still be wrong.

Antioch: the history first

Antioch pushed the other way. Diodore of Tarsus, Theodore of Mopsuestia, St John Chrysostom and Theodoret insisted that the plain historical sense comes first, and that the spiritual meaning must grow out of it rather than replace it. Their word for that contemplative reading was theoria, a seeing through the history rather than past it.

Chrysostom's homilies show what the approach looks like in a parish. He works verse by verse, explains the customs, notices who is speaking to whom, and then turns without warning to the congregation in front of him and asks what they intend to do about the poor man outside the door. It is exegesis for people who will be at work in the morning.

Here too the reception was uneven. Theodore of Mopsuestia, the most rigorous of the Antiochenes, was condemned at the Fifth Ecumenical Council in 553. Chrysostom is one of the three Holy Hierarchs and the most widely read preacher in the Orthodox world. The Church kept the method and did not keep every conclusion reached by everyone who used it.

Why the Church kept both

The two emphases are often presented as rivals, and modern scholars have increasingly qualified how sharp the division really was. Alexandrians could be literal and Antiochenes could be figural, and the same writer frequently does both on one page. Still, the tension is genuine, and the interesting thing is that the Church never resolved it by choosing.

She kept both because each corrects the other. Without the Antiochene discipline, allegory becomes a machine for finding whatever the reader brought with him. Without the Alexandrian eye, the Bible flattens into ancient history with morals attached. Holding the two together is uncomfortable, and the discomfort is much of the point: the text is being read by people who expect it to exceed them.

Consensus is not unanimity

A common misunderstanding needs clearing away here. Orthodoxy does not teach that every Father is right about everything, or that they agree verse by verse. They do not. They contradict one another on chronology, on the identity of minor figures, on the meaning of particular passages and occasionally on more than that. What the Church looks for is the consensus patrum: agreement that is ancient, widespread and consistent, on the things that carry weight.

St Vincent of Lérins described both halves of this. His famous rule is to hold the faith “which has been believed everywhere, always, by all”. Less famously, in the same book, he insists that real progress in understanding is not merely permitted but required, so long as it is growth “in the same doctrine, in the same sense, and in the same meaning” rather than a change into something else.

Reading a Father is therefore a skill and not simply an act of submission. Fr. Seraphim Rose, an American convert raised in a Protestant family, wrote a whole book to demonstrate what that looks like. The Place of Blessed Augustine in the Orthodox Church argues against two currents at once: an inflated estimate of Augustine drawn from Western sources, and a fashion among some Orthodox writers of the 1970s for calling him a heretic. What he asked for instead was neither.

a balanced appraisal of him with due credit given both to his unquestioned greatness and to his faults

Fr. Seraphim Rose, The Place of Blessed Augustine in the Orthodox Church (St. Herman of Alaska Brotherhood, 1983), ch. I, p. 8

Reading a Father critically, and reverently

Fr. Seraphim did not defend Augustine's doctrines of grace and predestination. He described himself elsewhere as no great admirer of them and thought them overly logical. What he defended was the man's piety, the contrition and love for Christ that fill the Confessions, and he judged that demolishing Augustine's reputation would take that piety down along with it.

The rule cuts in both directions, and it should be turned on modern teachers as readily as on ancient ones. A reviewer in the journal Orthodox Tradition called Fr. Seraphim's treatment of Augustine more balanced than most, and in the same notice pointed out that he had misattributed one patristic reference and that, working largely from Russian sources, his citations of the Greek Fathers are not always faithful to the Greek. Reading a teacher reverently includes checking him.

For an enquirer this is freeing rather than unsettling. Nobody is asking you to accept a body of writing uncritically, and nobody in the Orthodox tradition ever has. What is asked is that you read with the Church rather than against her, assume that people who prayed for decades saw things you have not yet seen, and hold your own conclusions a little more loosely than the accumulated judgement of centuries.

I myself fear the cold hearts of the 'intellectually correct' much more than any errors you might find in Augustine.

Fr. Seraphim Rose, letter to Fr. Michael, 26 June 1981, in Hieromonk Damascene, Father Seraphim Rose: His Life and Works (St. Herman Press, 2003), ch. 86

The heart before the mind

One further element belongs here, and in the Fathers it comes first rather than last. They did not think Scripture yields its meaning to cleverness. Purification precedes illumination: the reader is expected to be praying, fasting, confessing and giving alms, on the understanding that the obstacle to understanding is more often the condition of the reader than the difficulty of the text.

Fr. Seraphim put the same conviction to a room of Californian students in the spring of 1981, in a lecture that became a small book. He had spent years in Eastern philosophy before he came to Christ, and what he told them was not a technique.

Whether that claim is true is something each reader has to settle somewhere other than in an article. But it explains a good deal about Orthodox practice: why the Psalter is put into the hands of beginners while the harder commentaries wait, and why a monk who has read very little is not regarded as a poor reader of Scripture.

In practice all of this comes to something unremarkable. The Orthodox Christian reads the Bible daily from a fixed lectionary shared with every other Orthodox parish that day. He hears it sung and chanted at the services, where most of the hymns are themselves stitched out of biblical phrases. He reads a commentary, Chrysostom on Matthew being the usual starting place, and he asks his priest when he is stuck.

None of that substitutes for reading; it is a way of not reading alone. The difference from a private devotional habit is not that one side is serious and the other is not. Many Protestant readers put Orthodox Christians to shame in sheer diligence with the text, and Fr. Seraphim said as much about the Protestants he knew and admired. The difference lies in the assumption that the book belongs to a community stretched across twenty centuries, and that the community's reading has a claim on mine.

That claim can be declined, and declining it is a coherent position held by sincere Christians for reasons they can state. If you would rather test it than argue about it, the simplest experiment is to read one Gospel for a month alongside Chrysostom's homilies on it and see what happens. Our parish in Kovan will lend you a copy and ask nothing in return.

God's revelation is given to something called a loving heart.

Fr. Seraphim Rose, God's Revelation to the Human Heart (St. Herman of Alaska Brotherhood, 2nd edn, 2014), §2 'Revelation'

Common questions

Do Orthodox Christians believe the Church Fathers were infallible?

No. The Fathers disagree with one another on many points and no individual Father is held to be free from error. What Orthodoxy trusts is the consensus patrum: agreement that is ancient, widespread and consistent on matters of substance. St Vincent of Lérins gave the test in 434, namely what has been believed everywhere, always and by all. Individual opinions outside that consensus are read with respect and with judgement.

What is typology?

Reading a person, event or object in the Old Testament as a real historical thing which also prefigures Christ. The New Testament does this constantly: St Paul calls Israel's wilderness experiences types (1 Corinthians 10:11), St Peter reads the flood as a figure of baptism (1 Peter 3:21), and the Lord reads the bronze serpent and Jonah as figures of his Cross and burial. The Fathers extended a method they had inherited from the apostles.

What is the difference between the Alexandrian and Antiochene schools?

Broadly, Alexandria emphasised the spiritual meaning beneath the letter while Antioch insisted that the historical sense comes first. Modern scholars have qualified the contrast, since writers on both sides did both. The Orthodox Church never chose between them: Cyril of Alexandria and John Chrysostom are both great teachers. Each emphasis restrains the other's characteristic failure, whether allegory untethered from the text or history with nothing beyond it.

Can I read the Bible on my own?

Yes, and Orthodox Christians are expected to. The daily lectionary exists precisely so that everyone reads. Reading with the Church means reading with help and with a check: hearing the text at the services, reading a Father such as Chrysostom alongside it, and asking a priest when a passage resists. The aim is not to restrict reading but to keep it from drifting without anyone noticing that it has.

Where should someone begin with the Fathers?

St John Chrysostom's homilies on Matthew or on Genesis are the usual first recommendation, since he is clear, practical and addressed to ordinary congregations. St Athanasius' On the Incarnation is short and formative. After those, St Basil's Hexaemeron and the Confessions of Blessed Augustine. Read slowly, and alongside the biblical text rather than instead of it, which is how the Fathers themselves expected to be used.
